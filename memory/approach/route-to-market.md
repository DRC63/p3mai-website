# Route to Market — the approach behind the site and the apps

**Status:** Active guiding framework, adopted August 2026.
**Source:** `reference/Book 5 - Making Money with Claude - Summary.docx` (30-page branded summary of
Book 5, pp. 183–219, with 25 original diagrams and AED/GBP conversion tables).
**Use it for:** any decision about what to build next, how to price it, how to package it, and how
to find the first customers for it — website, apps, services, content.

## The core model — the revenue stack

Four layers, each feeding the others. This is the spine of the plan.

| Layer | What it is | Role |
|-------|-----------|------|
| 1 | **Content** | Widest part of the funnel. Little direct revenue; attracts attention and proves expertise. |
| 2 | **Digital products** | Passive income *and* a qualifier — a paying buyer is a likelier consulting client than a follower. |
| 3 | **Freelancing & automation** | Primary early revenue. Systematise so the fifth client takes half the time of the first. |
| 4 | **Consulting** | Premium. Fewer clients, higher rates; engagements loop back to Layer 3 at premium rates. |

Content attracts clients → client work becomes case studies → case studies attract consulting →
consulting insight becomes content and products. The flywheel is the point, not any single layer.

**Where P3MAI currently sits:** Layers 2–4 are built or buildable (apps as products, consulting as
the offer, the site as the shopfront). **Layer 1 — content — is the gap.** That is the constraint
on inbound, not the offering.

## Principles that govern build decisions

- **Price on value delivered, not hours spent.** Anchor at 20–30% of the client's saving. An
  automation saving 20 hrs/week at $50/hr saves them $4,000/month; $1,000/month is a bargain to
  them and good revenue here.
- **Specificity sells.** A named profession with a named problem beats a general capability.
  "AI services for businesses" is a trap — nobody searches for it. This applies to app naming,
  page headlines and service cards.
- **Validate before over-investing.** Build the smallest version, put it in front of someone, see
  if anyone bites. Get paid first, professionalise later.
- **Productise what repeats.** When the same request appears three times, package it at a fixed
  price with a defined scope.
- **Always offer three tiers.** Basic / standard / premium. Most choose the middle — design the
  middle to be the profitable one. Applies to services *and* to app pricing.
- **Human review step stays in every automation**, even where it could run autonomously. Clients
  buy the safety net; it also protects against a bad output going live.
- **One primary content platform, not five.** For this buyer (programme directors, PMO leads,
  enterprise) that is **LinkedIn**.
- **The competitor is inaction**, not other experts. Most target businesses are doing nothing.

## Reusable delivery workflows

**Client delivery (6 steps):** brief → build a client-specific skill → first draft (you are the
quality filter) → review with *specific* questions → revisions that also update the skill →
deliver and propose the next step.

**Automation delivery (5 steps):** 30-min discovery call → 1–2 hr solution design + one-page
proposal → build → 1 hr client training (this is what makes it get used) → handover with monthly
check-ins for three months, then quarterly.

**Consulting engagement (4 phases):** discovery (1–2 wks) → strategy (1–2 wks) → implementation
support (ongoing) → review and optimisation (quarterly). Each phase is a checkpoint where the
client chooses to continue.

## Sales assets to maintain

1. **One-paragraph pitch** — "I help [client] [solve problem] using AI-powered [solution], so they
   can [benefit]." Must work verbatim in a LinkedIn message.
2. **One-page service overview (PDF)** — service, problem, what they get, process and timeline,
   pricing, contact.
3. **Case studies** — problem, approach, solution, measurable results. Three strong ones outweigh a
   hundred posts. Ask for the two-sentence testimonial explicitly at delivery.
4. **Free audit** — 30 minutes, identify 3–5 quick wins plus 2–3 strategic opportunities. The quick
   wins prove value; the strategic ones become the proposal.

## Operating rhythm

Target split: **60% delivery · 20% marketing and content · 10% product development · 10% admin and
learning.** Never 100% delivery, or the inbound never builds. Two hours a week reserved for staying
current (release notes, practitioner communities, testing new features) — that doubles as content
material.

## Applying this to the existing estate

- **The site (p3mai.com)** is the one-page-service-overview and case-study layer. Lead with strategy
  and outcomes, not a list of capabilities — that is the freelancer-to-consultant positioning shift.
- **The apps (`apps.p3mai.com/*`)** are Layer 2 digital products. Each should have a named audience,
  a named problem, and eventually a three-tier price. The P3M3 assessment is the natural free
  audit / lead magnet — it identifies gaps, which is exactly what a paid engagement then fixes.
- **The calculator** is a qualifier — it puts a number in front of a prospect before contact.
- **Next gap:** the content layer, on LinkedIn, drawing on real delivery experience (Core42
  supercomputers, RBS, the PMO builds). Specific, results-led posts — not general AI commentary.
